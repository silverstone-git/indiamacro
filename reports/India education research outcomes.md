# India education research outcomes

*Trace India's research-to-outcomes chain*

India's education-and-R&D chain now has measurable links at every stage but no measured conversion between them: official R&D intensity reached **0.84% of GDP in 2023-24**, researcher density reached **354 full-time-equivalent researchers per million in 2023-24**, journal articles and patent filings are at record volumes, and rural Std V reading recovered to **48.8% in 2024** — yet no free public source connects a specific rupee to a citation, a licensed patent, a viable firm or a child's learning gain. This draft follows the money-in → capacity → outputs → outcomes chain in [the plan](../docs/RESEARCH_OUTCOME_ANGLE.md), India first and peers only as dated context, using free/public data and naming paid data only as future gaps. It keeps conflicting series apart rather than averaging them: **0.84% versus World Bank 0.646%**, **354 versus 259 researchers per million**, **4.06% versus 2.7% education spending**, **45.2% versus 51.8% private R&D share**, **AISHE GER 30.0 versus UIS 34.45**, and patent-office filings versus India-origin counts. Each number carries its producer, definition and observation year; estimates are labelled estimates and vintages are labelled vintages. The intended reader will cite these numbers, so the honest headline is "rapid expansion with unmeasured conversions", not "money well spent" and not "money wasted".

Every web link in this report was **accessed on 20 Nov 2026**; the underlying observation year is printed with each figure. Citation wording for students: "National Statistics Office/ DST-NSTMIS, Pratham/ASER, WIPO, Indian Patent Office, World Bank, Ministry of Education, PIB or OpenAlex, accessed via the producer's site or API on 20 Nov 2026", as appropriate — not a citation of this package.

## What the plan proposed and what this draft does

The plan dated **19 Nov 2026** proposed new free-data modules for ASER, UDISE+/NAS, Union Budget R&D and education lines, patents, startups and OpenAlex, plus dashboard pages that line up input, capacity, output and outcome; it also listed paid or login-gated datasets to request later ([plan](../docs/RESEARCH_OUTCOME_ANGLE.md)). A local audit saved with the research notes found that the library currently has none of those modules, but that `compare()` already accepts raw World Bank codes with `source="worldbank"`, so the plan's first step needs no new comparison function. This report is research synthesis only: it adds **no source module, dashboard page, test or data file** to the repository, and it does not use the unrelated conversation notes in [docs/econ_ai_report_slop.md](../docs/econ_ai_report_slop.md) as evidence. Following [CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md), it cites original producers, states what is free and what is paid, and leaves all private/paid-data analysis for the later report the user has already planned.

## Money in: research intensity stays flat while budgets swing

India's official R&D effort is much larger in rupees but hardly closer to the world's research-intensive economies. DST/NSTMIS's *R&D Statistics at a Glance 2025-26* reports GERD of **₹2,44,767.81 crore in 2023-24**, more than triple the **₹79,355.89 crore in 2013-14**, with the ratio reaching **0.84% of GDP "for the first time"**; the same chart prints **0.71% in 1990-91**, so the 2023-24 peak is only about 0.13 percentage points above the level three decades earlier ([DST/NSTMIS](https://dst.gov.in/static/uploads/2026/07/ab7aa62b590dcc3a18f6dabde045b5a0.pdf)). The **0.87% for 2024-25 and 0.90% for 2025-26 in that publication are estimates**, not observations, and must be labelled as such. Peer values from the same World Bank indicator for 2023 are **Korea 4.94%, USA 3.45%, Germany 3.15%, China 2.58% and Brazil 1.19%**; the government's own stated benchmark is **2% of GDP by 2035** ([World Bank API, GB.XPD.RSDV.GD.ZS](https://api.worldbank.org/v2/country/IND;CHN;KOR;USA;DEU;BRA/indicator/GB.XPD.RSDV.GD.ZS?format=json&per_page=20000), [DST parliamentary answer, 6 Aug 2026](https://sansad.in/getFile/annex/271/AU2204_8RZMVe.pdf?source=pqars)).

| R&D intensity figure | Value | Date and definition | How to use it |
|---|---:|---|---|
| DST/NSTMIS official GERD | **0.84%** (₹2,44,767.81 cr) | FY2023-24; survey-based National S&T Survey 2024-25, Frascati-aligned, supplemented by annual reports, BRSR, NIRF, AISHE, DSIR records and **CMIE** (paid inputs to a free aggregate) | Latest official India value |
| World Bank WDI `GB.XPD.RSDV.GD.ZS` | **0.646%** | 2020; UNESCO/UIS compilation, API metadata updated 11 Nov 2026 | Latest *internationally compiled* value; **stale**, not "latest India" |
| DST projections | 0.87%, 0.90% | FY2024-25, FY2025-26 | **Estimates**, excluded from observed series |

The 0.84% and 0.646% figures are a **coverage/vintage break, not two rival estimates of the same year**: World Bank 2019 is **0.659%**, numerically consistent with DST's **0.66% in 2019-20**, and the World Bank series simply stops after 2020 ([World Bank API](https://api.worldbank.org/v2/country/IND;CHN;KOR;USA;DEU;BRA/indicator/GB.XPD.RSDV.GD.ZS?format=json&per_page=20000), [DST, 6 Aug 2026](https://sansad.in/getFile/annex/271/AU2204_8RZMVe.pdf?source=pqars)). No source consulted publishes a formal reconciliation of fiscal- versus calendar-year denominators.

Public education spending has the same problem in a sharper form: three defensible series answer three different questions.

| Series | Latest value | What it covers |
|---|---:|---|
| World Bank/UNESCO `SE.XPD.TOTL.GD.ZS` | **4.10% of GDP (2022)** | Government expenditure on education; India's earlier points include 3.38% (2010) and 4.63% (2021) ([World Bank API](https://api.worldbank.org/v2/country/IND;CHN;KOR;USA;DEU;BRA/indicator/SE.XPD.TOTL.GD.ZS?format=json&per_page=20000)) |
| Ministry of Education, *Analysis of Budgeted Expenditure on Education* | **4.06% of GDP (2022-23)** | Centre + States/UTs; the same budget-based series gives 4.12% for 2021-22 **BE** and 4.36% for 2020-21 **RE**, so stages are mixed ([Lok Sabha UNQ 2642, 9 Mar 2026](https://sansad.in/getFile/loksabhaquestions/annex/187/AU2642_2m08h4.pdf?source=pqals), [Rajya Sabha UNQ 1993, 17 Dec 2025](https://sansad.in/getFile/annex/269/AU1993_wjzj6C.pdf?source=pqars)) |
| Economic Survey 2025-26 / RBI | **2.7% of GDP (FY2026 BE)** | General government head explicitly defined as **"Education, Sports, Art and Culture"**, current market-price GDP with 2011-12 base ([Economic Survey, Ch. 13](https://www.indiabudget.gov.in/economicsurvey/doc/eschapter/echap13.pdf)) |

A single undated "India spends X% on education" is therefore unusable for citation. On every definition the **NEP 2020 goal of 6% of GDP "at the earliest" remains unmet**, with a gap of roughly 1.9–3.3 percentage points depending on series; no dated bridge plan was found ([Lok Sabha UNQ 2642](https://sansad.in/getFile/loksabhaquestions/annex/187/AU2642_2m08h4.pdf?source=pqals)). Peer comparisons are indicative only because the latest years range from 2021 to 2023.

Who performs and funds R&D produces another apparent contradiction that dissolves under definitions. DST's **45.2% private share** is *private industry only*; the **51.8% private share** in the parliamentary answer defines "Private Sector" as private industry **plus SIROs plus private higher-education institutions**, with government at 48.2% ([NSTMIS 2025-26, Chart 3](https://dst.gov.in/static/uploads/2026/07/ab7aa62b590dcc3a18f6dabde045b5a0.pdf), [PIB, 29 Jul 2026](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2291139)). Both are official; the roughly 6.6-point difference is the broader private boundary, and averaging them would be wrong. Within central government R&D, 12 agencies account for 76% (DRDO 32.7%, Department of Space 15.0%, DAE 14.4%, ICAR 13.1%, CSIR 8.7%); **MNCs account for 71.3% of *business* R&D**, which is not the same claim as "foreign firms fund Indian R&D" ([NSTMIS 2025-26, Charts 4 and 7](https://dst.gov.in/static/uploads/2026/07/ab7aa62b590dcc3a18f6dabde045b5a0.pdf)). Below the sector aggregate, free data stop.

The budget is the cleanest free "allocation versus actual" evidence, but it must be read at the right level. Union Budget Statement 3 shows DST at **₹5,021.41 crore actual in 2024-25, ₹28,508.90 crore BE in 2025-26, ₹11,921.58 crore RE and ₹28,049.32 crore BE in 2026-27**; the jump is dominated by a **₹20,000 crore RDI scheme capital provision** in both BEs, not by a six-fold expansion of ordinary science spending ([India Budget, Statement 3](https://www.indiabudget.gov.in/doc/eb/stat3a.pdf), [Demand 89 notes](https://www.indiabudget.gov.in/doc/eb/sbe89.pdf)). The Standing Committee reported DST utilisation of about **59.95% of RE in 2025-26**, **zero actual RDI expenditure as of 31 Jan 2026** against a ₹20,000 crore BE, and ANRF utilisation of **₹1,191 crore of ₹1,948 crore RE** ([PIB/Lok Sabha Secretariat, 25 Mar 2026](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2245310&lang=2&reg=48)). These are budget-mechanics and execution findings. Outstanding utilisation certificates — **41,526 certificates covering ₹16,905.17 crore** in the CAG's 2025 report and **41,002 covering ₹16,761.41 crore** in 2026 — mean documentation was not furnished, **not** that grants were embezzled ([CAG Report No. 29 of 2025](https://cag.gov.in/webroot/uploads/download_audit_report/2025/DGA-ESD-Scientific-and-Environmental-Report-29-ENGLISH-of-2025-FINAL-FOR-Net-uploading--%2818-12-2025%29-06943fa76e40574.33513964.pdf), [CAG Report No. 17 of 2026](https://cag.gov.in/webroot/uploads/download_audit_report/2026/17-of-2026-Eng-06a82a8d2895718.95052747.pdf)). The **ANRF ₹50,000 crore mobilisation target (₹14,000 crore Centre)** and the **₹1 lakh crore RDI scheme over six years** are multi-year envelopes; private contributions so far are letters of intent, not realised receipts ([PIB, 4 Nov 2025](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2186327&noshow=1), [PIB, 29 Jul 2026](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2291131&lang=2&reg=48)).

## Capacity expands, but senior academic posts remain vacant

India's research workforce is growing quickly from a very low base, and the two available series cannot be spliced. As at **1 April 2024**, DST counted **5.07 lakh FTE researchers (507,351)**, of whom 147,119 were women (29%), and **354 per million population**, up from 262 in 2020-21; higher education employed **238,728 of 507,351 FTE R&D staff**, about 47% ([DST/NSTMIS](https://dst.gov.in/static/uploads/2026/07/ab7aa62b590dcc3a18f6dabde045b5a0.pdf), [S&T Indicators Tables 2025-26](https://www.nstmis-dst.org/Pdfs/ST%20INDICATORS%20TABLES%202025-26.pdf)). The internationally compiled World Bank/UNESCO series instead ends at **259.263 researchers per million in 2020**, with eight Indian observations over 1996–2020 ([World Bank API, SP.POP.SCIE.RD.P6](https://api.worldbank.org/v2/country/IND;CHN;KOR;USA;DEU;BRA/indicator/SP.POP.SCIE.RD.P6?format=json&per_page=20000)). DST's own peer table labels 354 as "2023" while its narrative says 2023-24; **354 (2023-24, DST) and 259 (2020, UNESCO/WB) are different vintages and definitions**, not a trend break to be smoothed. On the common year 2020, China was 1,600 per million and Korea 8,620; at DST's 2023 comparison Korea was about 27 times India. Technicians are a weaker stub: India's last World Bank value is **73.06 per million in 2018**, with no values for China or the USA in that window, so no current comparison is possible ([World Bank API, SP.POP.TECH.RD.P6](https://api.worldbank.org/v2/country/IND;CHN;KOR;USA;DEU;BRA/indicator/SP.POP.TECH.RD.P6?format=json&per_page=20000)).

Higher education is large in headcount and still low in participation. AISHE 2023-24, the latest final report, records **4.50 crore students in 59,553 responding institutions** (1,278 universities, 46,468 colleges, 11,787 standalone institutions), **17.3 lakh teachers** and a GER of **30.0** computed on a 2011-census population projection; published totals pool 2021-22 and 2022-23 responses because actual 2023-24 responses were lower ([AISHE Final Report 2023-24](https://cdnbbsr.s3waas.gov.in/s392049debbe566ca5782a3045cf300a3c/uploads/2026/07/202607131602421770.pdf)). UIS/WB reports **34.45 in 2025** (34.24 in 2023) using its own enrolment and population denominators, so the ~4-point gap is a **definition conflict to disclose, not a number to average** ([World Bank API, SE.TER.ENRR](https://api.worldbank.org/v2/country/IND;CHN;KOR;USA;DEU;BRA/indicator/SE.TER.ENRR?format=json&per_page=20000)). The same AISHE report gives **two PhD enrolment figures — 3,43,559 in the narrative and 2,92,552 in Box 13** — and puts PhD awards at **37,601** on an actual-response, two-convocation definition; that is not a cohort completion rate ([AISHE 2023-24](https://cdnbbsr.s3waas.gov.in/s392049debbe566ca5782a3045cf300a3c/uploads/2026/07/202607131602421770.pdf)). School capacity is near-universal on inputs: UDISE+ 2024-25 reports **14.71 lakh schools, 1.01 crore teachers and 24.69 crore students**, with pupil–teacher ratios of 20 in primary and 15 in secondary, but warns that data from 2022-23 are **not strictly comparable with 2021-22 or earlier** ([UDISE+ 2024-25 Booklet](https://dashboard.udiseplus.gov.in/report2026/static/media/UDISE+2024_25_Booklet_existing.118ba29d4773e6372f72.pdf)).

The constraint visible in official evidence is at the senior academic end, not in enrolment. The Parliamentary Standing Committee's 364th Report found **5,410 of 18,940 sanctioned teaching posts vacant (28.56%) in central universities as at 31 Dec 2024**, rising to **56.18% of professorships as at 31 Jan 2025**, and flagged equipment and lab-input costs ([364th Report, March 2025](https://sansad.in/getFile/rsnew/Committee_site/Committee_File/ReportFile/16/198/364_2025_3_15.pdf?source=rajyasabha)). This corroborates the researcher-stock picture — a growing junior pipeline meeting a large unfilled senior posts share — but it is a vacancy count, not proof of a specific hiring or funding mechanism. A targeted DST evaluation of FIST found 2,620 equipment items procured and used in 581 southern-India projects sanctioned over 2000–2011; it is evidence that funded access worked in that programme, not a measured national shortfall ([NSTMIS FIST evaluation](http://digitalrepository-nstmis-dst.org/pcr/es/308/308/Executive-Summary.pdf)).

## Outputs surge while measured impact stays below the world average

India's publication volume is now comparable to the USA's in level and faster growing, but its citation-normalised impact remains below the world average and below every rich-country peer in the comparison set. The World Bank/UNESCO series counts **228,174 scientific and technical journal articles for India in 2023**; OpenAlex counts **255,226 `type:article` works** and **388,686 works of all types** for the same country and year, because corpora and whole- versus fractional-counting rules differ ([World Bank API, IP.JRN.ARTC.SC](https://api.worldbank.org/v2/country/IND;CHN;KOR;USA;DEU;BRA/indicator/IP.JRN.ARTC.SC?format=json&per_page=20000), [OpenAlex API, articles](https://api.openalex.org/works?filter=institutions.country_code:IN,publication_year:2023,type:article&select=id&per-page=1), [OpenAlex API, all works](https://api.openalex.org/works?filter=institutions.country_code:IN,publication_year:2023&select=id&per-page=1)). OpenAlex's field-normalised proxy shows **76,059 of 255,226 articles (29.8%) with FWCI > 1 in 2023**, versus 49.3% for China, 45.3% for the USA, 53.9% for the UK, 48.5% for Germany and 43.6% for Korea; FWCI is citations over the publication year plus three years divided by the world average for the same year, type and subfield ([OpenAlex, FWCI > 1](https://api.openalex.org/works?filter=institutions.country_code:IN,publication_year:2023,type:article,fwci:%3E1&select=id&per-page=1), [OpenAlex citation documentation](https://help.openalex.org/data/works/citations.md)). A free publisher summary of paid Clarivate data puts India's **CNCI at 0.89 in 2024**, up from 0.73 in 2015 — a different corpus and normalisation, so its rise alongside a slightly falling OpenAlex FWCI share is a **methodology-dependent disagreement, not a resolved trend** ([Springer Nature, *Global Research Pulse: India*](https://stories.springernature.com/global-research-pulse-india/)). None of these measures social usefulness.

Patents show the same divide between activity and value, with even sharper definition traps.

| Measure | Indian Patent Office (fiscal year, office data) | WIPO WIPI 2026 (calendar 2025, by origin worldwide) | Reading |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Applications | **110,375 in FY2024-25** (68,201 domestic, 42,174 foreign) | **105,163 in 2025**, +37.5%, first year above 100,000 | Different universes: filings *received at the Indian office* versus applications *from India-based applicants globally* |
| Grants | **33,504 in FY2024-25** (10,682 to Indian applicants) | **15,989 India-origin grants in 2025**, −41.2% | Not comparable; grants lag applications and move with examination capacity |
| Patents in force | **230,480 as at 1 Apr 2025** (50,848 Indian) | **224,162 in 2025** | Different dates and coverage; do not difference them |

Sources: [IPO Annual Report 2024-25](https://www.ipindia.gov.in/storage/uploads/docs-operator/7a8f0ca4-5a8e-416e-b8d4-5647ec352bb9.pdf) and [WIPO WIPI 2026, tables A4/A36](https://www.wipo.int/edocs/pubdocs/en/wipo-pub-941-2026-en-world-intellectual-property-indicators-2026.pdf), both accessed 20 Nov 2026. WIPO itself warns against comparing same-year applications with grants, and notes that grant shares across offices are not directly comparable; its **77.7% Indian office grant share for 2025** is an examination-outcome statistic, not a technology-quality score ([WIPO WIPI 2026, pp. 18–19](https://www.wipo.int/edocs/pubdocs/en/wipo-pub-941-2026-en-world-intellectual-property-indicators-2026.pdf)). The clearest free input-to-output link is that Indian educational institutions filed **37,681 applications in FY2024-25 — 55% of the 68,201 domestic filings and 34% of all 110,375 applications received at the Indian office** (the latter total includes 42,174 foreign filings); the IPO report also names the top academic filers. This establishes participation in the formal IP system, **not** commercial value, licensing or maintenance ([IPO Annual Report 2024-25, pp. 21–23](https://www.ipindia.gov.in/storage/uploads/docs-operator/7a8f0ca4-5a8e-416e-b8d4-5647ec352bb9.pdf)). Forward citations, family size, triadic families and cohort renewal/lapse rates are **not available as free India-level aggregates** from the sources checked; PatentsView/USPTO Open Data is US-centric and key-gated, while OECD triadic-family coverage for India was not verified ([USPTO transition guide](https://data.uspto.gov/support/transition-guide/patentsview), [OECD triadic families methodology](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2004/03/triadic-patent-families-methodology_g17a156f/443844125004.pdf)).

The startup link is a participation indicator, not an outcome measure. DPIIT had **2.23 lakh recognised startups as at 31 Mar 2026**, with 55,200+ recognised in FY2025-26; the Factbook states that certificate counts include approved, **cancelled and expired** startups and that its jobs figure is **self-reported by startups**, so "2.23 lakh successful startups" and "23.36 lakh verified jobs" are not supportable ([PIB, 17 Apr 2026](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2253019&reg=48&lang=2), [Startup India 9-Year Factbook](https://www.startupindia.gov.in/content/dam/invest-india/startup-milestone/FactbookJan25.pdf)). The only official closure figure found — **6,385 dissolved or struck off among 1,97,692 recognised entities as at 31 Oct 2025** — is a stock in a cumulative register, not a cohort survival rate ([PIB, 2 Dec 2025](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleaseDetail.aspx?PRID=2197662&reg=6&lang=1)). Public scheme disbursements are better documented than private fundraising: the Fund of Funds for Startups had disbursed over ₹7,000 crore to 135+ AIFs by end-FY2025-26, which in turn invested over ₹26,900 crore in 1,420+ startups, and GeM orders to startups were ₹19,190 crore in FY2025-26 ([PIB, 17 Apr 2026](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2253019&reg=48&lang=2)). These are flows of money and procurement, not measures of firm viability.

## Learning rebounds, but the bridge from spending stays unproven

The best direct evidence on what children actually learn is rural and household-based, and it shows a real but incomplete recovery. ASER 2024 surveyed **649,491 children in 17,997 villages across 605 rural districts**; **48.8%** of rural Std V children could read a Std II-level text and **30.7%** could solve a three-digit-by-one-digit division problem, up from **42.8% and 25.6% in 2022** but with reading still **1.7 points below the 50.5% peak in 2018** ([ASER 2024 report](https://asercentre.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/ASER_2024_Final-Report_13_2_24-1.pdf)). Government-school reading rose from 38.5% to 44.8% over 2022–24, while private-school reading rose from 56.8% to 59.3%, still below 65.1% in 2018; ASER cautions that household self-selection means the gap cannot be read as a pure school effect. The comparable trend starts in **2006 for reading and 2007 for arithmetic**: ASER excludes 2005 because of different sampling and 2006 arithmetic because the tool changed, and the 2020–21 phone surveys and the Census 2001→2011 village-frame change break district-level comparability ([ASER Trends Over Time](https://img.asercentre.org/docs/Publications/ASER%20Reports/ASER%20TOT/fullasertrendsovertimereport.pdf), [ASER 2024 FAQ/Annexure 13](https://asercentre.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Annexure_13.pdf)).

Official school-based assessments are a different construct and should never be plotted on the same scale. PARAKH's Rashtriya Sarvekshan 2024 tested grades 3, 6 and 9 on **4 Dec 2024** across 74,229 schools and 21.15 lakh students; its state report cards put national Grade 3 Language/Mathematics at **64%/60%**, Grade 6 at **57%/46%** and Grade 9 Language/Mathematics at **54%/37%**, with Grade 3 Language ranging from **82% in Punjab to 56% in Bihar** ([PARAKH PRS 2024](https://parakh.ncert.gov.in/prs), [state cards](https://parakh.ncert.gov.in/prs-reports-2024-statelist)). The Economic Survey's claim that Grade III rose from 39%/42% in NAS 2021 to **57%/65% in PARAKH 2024** conflicts with those state cards and is not used as the 2024 headline ([Economic Survey 2025-26, Ch. 11](https://www.indiabudget.gov.in/economicsurvey/doc/eschapter/echap11.pdf)). The cards also print that mean differences were not statistically tested.

International learning comparators are older still. India's **HCI 2020 is 0.494** and **LAYS is 7.10 years in 2020** (against China's 9.27 and Brazil's 7.87); the 2026 HCI+ release scores India **159 out of 325**, with tertiary completion, harmonised learning outcomes and expected years of schooling named as the largest gaps with China ([World Bank HCI indicator](https://api.worldbank.org/v2/country/IND;BGD;PAK;CHN;BRA;IDN;ZAF/indicator/HD.HCI.OVRL?format=json&per_page=200), [World Bank LAYS](https://api.worldbank.org/v2/country/IND;BGD;PAK;CHN;BRA;IDN;ZAF/indicator/HD.HCI.LAYS?format=json&per_page=200), [India HCI+ brief](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099841307032634415/pdf/IDU-b6ea9ac1-a7a9-4e54-82e1-92b54ec783d7.pdf)). HCI, HCI+ and LAYS are different vintages, not one trend. India has **no nationally representative PISA sample** in PISA 2022, so ASER, NAS and PARAKH are the domestic evidence base. The ASER gains coincided with a nationwide foundational-literacy push — 83% of sampled schools reported an FLN directive and 78% an FLN-trained teacher in 2024 — but the design is observational; South Africa spending about 6.2% of GDP in 2020 with LAYS 5.60 while India spent about 4.3% with LAYS 7.10 is a descriptive comparison, not evidence of efficiency ([ASER 2024 report](https://asercentre.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/ASER_2024_Final-Report_13_2_24-1.pdf), [World Bank WDR 2018](https://www.worldbank.org/en/publication/wdr2018)).

## What this draft does not claim

| Claim | Why it is out of scope here |
|---|---|
| "X% of Indian patents or startups are useful/successful/valuable" | No free source measures a fraction of *useful* patents or startups; grants, citations, registrations and GeM orders are different things. |
| "R&D money was wasted" or "X papers per rupee" | No source links a specific rupee to a specific paper, patent or firm; budget data show allocation and utilisation, never intent or attribution. |
| "India has 2.23 lakh successful startups" or "23.36 lakh verified jobs" | Recognition is self-declared, certificates include cancelled and expired entities, and jobs are self-reported. |
| "Spending or NEP/FLN caused the 2022–24 ASER gains" | Spending, programme coverage and learning are documented separately, with no design isolating the intervention. |
| "A BE→RE cut or an outstanding utilisation certificate shows misuse" | These show budget mechanics and missing documentation; misuse findings require CAG audit conclusions, which are not asserted here. |
| "The 41.2% fall in India-origin grants means inventions got worse", "only ~3% of startups fail", or "university patents are low quality because universities file most" | Grant timing, cumulative registration stocks and filing counts do not support these inferences. |
| "India is above world average because it ranks 9th in Nature Index" | Nature Index is a selected-journal share and is compatible with CNCI 0.89 and a 29.8% OpenAlex FWCI>1 share. |

## Conclusion

The chain is now measurable as a set of dated checkpoints, and that alone changes the question: the interesting gap is no longer "is India doing research?" but "which conversions are unobserved?" The free record shows rising inputs, a large but thinly staffed capacity base, high-volume outputs with below-average citation impact, and a learning recovery that cannot be assigned to the spending that preceded it. Until firm-level R&D, patent legal status, startup cohorts and education microdata are added, the defensible product for students is a chain of clearly labelled proxies with the breaks left visible — not a composite score and not a verdict on whether the money worked.
